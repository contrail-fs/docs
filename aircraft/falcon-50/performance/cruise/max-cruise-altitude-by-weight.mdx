---
title: "Max Cruise Altitude by Weight"
description: "How heavy you are decides how high you can fly. A quick-reference for picking a realistic cruise altitude in the Falcon 50, so you don't end up too high and too heavy."
---

A jet can only climb as high as its wings and engines will let it at the **weight it is flying right now**. The most common mistake in the simulator is to file for FL450 straight away, even at near-maximum takeoff weight. The aircraft simply does not have the thrust to hold that altitude until it has burned off enough fuel.

Three things decide how high you can cruise:

- **Weight** — heavier means lower. As you burn fuel you can step-climb higher.
- **Temperature** — warmer air means lower. A hot day costs you several thousand feet.
- **Speed** — at high altitude there is only a narrow band of usable speeds. Too slow and the wing buffets; too fast and you hit the Mach limit.

<Note>
  The Falcon 50's maximum operating altitude is **FL450**. The figures below are the highest level the engines can sustain at **Maximum Cruise Thrust** — they are a practical ceiling, not a hard limit.
</Note>

## How high can I cruise?

Find your current weight, read across to the day's temperature, and that is roughly the highest flight level you should plan for. Start lower and step up as you get lighter.

| Cruise weight | Cold day (ISA −10 °C) | Standard day (ISA) | Warm day (ISA +10 °C) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 40,000 lb (near max) | FL390 | FL370 | FL350 |
| 38,000 lb | FL410 | FL390 | FL370 |
| 36,000 lb | FL410 | FL390 | FL370 |
| 34,000 lb | FL430 | FL410 | FL390 |
| 32,000 lb | FL430 | FL410 | FL390 |
| 30,000 lb | FL450 | FL430 | FL410 |
| 28,000 lb | FL450 | FL430 | FL410 |
| 26,000 lb or less | FL450 | FL450 | FL430 |

<Tip>
  A simple rule of thumb: **near max weight, stay around FL350–FL390**. You can usually reach the FL430–FL450 band only once you are below about 30,000 lb. On a warm day, knock one or two levels off everything.
</Tip>

## Why speed matters too

Even at the right altitude, the weight you can carry depends on how fast you fly. The slower you cruise (down toward the long-range speed), the more weight the wing can support up high. Push the speed up and drag climbs faster than thrust can keep up, so your ceiling drops.

The table below shows the heaviest weight that can be held at **FL410 on a cold day (ISA −10 °C)** for a range of cruise speeds:

| Cruise speed | Max weight at FL410 |
| --- | --- |
| Mach 0.70 | 38,000 lb |
| Mach 0.73 | 37,700 lb |
| Mach 0.75 | 36,700 lb |
| Mach 0.78 | 34,400 lb |
| Mach 0.80 | 32,400 lb |
| Mach 0.83 | 27,700 lb |

In other words, if you are heavy and high you must also fly **slower** to stay there — but not so slow that the wing starts to buffet. This shrinking gap between the high-speed and low-speed limits as you climb is often called the "coffin corner," and it is exactly why flying too high and too heavy is to be avoided.
